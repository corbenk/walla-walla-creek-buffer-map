Today, proposed, possible future

Today · rules depend on location

Walla Walla’s listed creek buffers are generally 35 ft, with 50 ft on the listed Yellowhawk reach. Exceptions and shoreline rules apply. Unincorporated land follows County rules; College Place has its own code. The County’s tiered working GIS layer is not proof of an adopted rule.

Current City code · Table 6

Proposed · City of Walla Walla

The City draft proposes 100 ft per side plus a 15-ft building setback. It is not a blanket County rule. Mill Creek follows the Shoreline Master Program.

City draft · Table 6 & setback

Possible future · a what-if, not a forecast

The County’s planning charter includes a critical-areas update. That does not establish a future blanket 100-ft rule. If a wider buffer were adopted where you live, it could reduce space for new buildings, additions or a subdivision; the actual effect would depend on the adopted code and your site.

County project charter · page 5

Ask for local science, a parcel impact analysis and clear protections for existing homes before new rules are adopted.

After the Oct 14 hearing

Council listened. Options on the creek buffer come back in November.

Thank you to everyone who filled the room, called in or wrote. About 30 people spoke over nearly 80 minutes, in the room and online. Council did not vote on the ordinance. It asked staff to come back with options and a plan of action no earlier than the first November meeting, Wed Nov 4, and by the end of November.

What Council asked for

Options it can weigh.

One councilmember asked that the options show what each would cost, how it would be paid for, how likely it is to succeed, and what being out of compliance with the state’s Growth Management Act would mean for the City. Others raised the cost of a local study and the state deadline.

An idea staff raised

Adopt on time, take effect Dec 31, 2027.

Staff said the City could adopt by the state deadline but set the 100-foot buffer to take effect Dec 31, 2027. Permit applications filed before then would be reviewed under today’s 35- or 50-foot buffers. Council has not decided.

What staff plans

Outreach to owners in the buffer.

Staff said the City intends to send mailers or do other outreach to owners whose property may lie in the buffer, inviting them to talk with staff about their lot. The City has also expanded the FAQ on its Critical Areas page.

Questions worth asking

Send one with your email, or bring it to a community information session if the City holds one; a councilmember suggested it.

  • What exactly happens if the City adopts after Dec 31, 2026? State law says a city fewer than 12 months late on its critical-areas rules still counts as making “substantial progress” for state public-works and water-quality loans and grants, though some funding preferences go only to cities that are on time. Could staff put the full list of consequences in writing?
  • Are there local studies showing what our own creeks need? If not, what would one cost, how long would it take, and could the City, the County and College Place share it?
  • Would buffers tiered by creek, like the County’s own 35-, 50-, 75- and 100-foot map layer, meet the state’s requirements?
  • Under a Dec 31, 2027 effective date, what counts as an application? Would it cover an addition, an ADU, or rebuilding after a fire?
  • Can a home inside the buffer be rebuilt after a fire without the six-month limit in existing code?
  • Could an addition, garage or ADU on the side of a home away from the creek be allowed without a critical-areas report?
  • Will the City publish how many lots and homes the buffer touches? By our count from the City’s buffer map and County Assessor records, it is about 1,490 lots, about 1,200 of them homes.

Worth keeping in mind

  • More time is not a different rule. A later effective date gives owners time to plan; on its own, the width stays 100 feet.
  • A local study could come out either way. A councilmember noted it could even recommend more than 100 feet. Our creeks still deserve to be measured.
  • “We need a plan to vote for.” A councilmember’s words. Specific, kind comments help most: your lot, your street, the option you would support.
  • Everyone is working under a state deadline. Several councilmembers thanked staff for their work. So do we.

Cautionary tales from the record

Other towns drew this line first. Here is what they learned.

Talent, Oregon · 2021

Fire survivors could not rebuild behind a 35-foot creek buffer.

Six months after the Almeda Fire, eight to ten Talent families still could not rebuild because their lots sat in a floodway or the city's 35-foot creek buffer, and the old homes could not be grandfathered. The city moved to an emergency ordinance so those families could rebuild.

Source: KTVL, Mar. 4, 2021 ↗

Spokane Valley, Washington · 1998

$20,000 spent on a lot, then the permit window closed under a flat 250-foot rule.

After Spokane County adopted a flat 250-foot river buffer in 1996, a Valley pharmacist who had owned his riverfront lot since 1983 spent about $20,000 on a drainfield, utilities and plans for a log home, then missed the permit window. Two neighbors had permits for side-by-side homes denied.

Source: Spokesman-Review, Oct. 29, 1998 ↗

Thurston County, Washington · 2024–25

Sixteen months and a mitigation plan to replace an 800-square-foot house.

A family on 0.83 acres with an 800-square-foot 1930s house too small for them applied in April 2024 to replace it. The wetland buffer covered the existing footprint. Approval came 16 months later, conditioned on 3,500 square feet of plantings and five years of monitoring.

Source: Thurston County Hearing Examiner decision, Aug. 4, 2025 (family not named at their privacy)

Puyallup · Newcastle

Puyallup and Newcastle: how residents changed the outcome

Homeowners filled a Puyallup hearing; Newcastle wrote rebuild rights in. Read both stories and what other towns adopted instead.

Elsewhere in Washington →

None of these towns ended with no buffer. Where residents won relief, it came as a buffer that fit the lot, measured per creek, with rebuild rights written in. That is the ask here.

One lot on Home Avenue

A little over half an acre on Garrison Creek. About 80% of it inside the band.

The lot is on Home Avenue, one of about 1,490 city lots the proposed band touches. Today the creek buffer there is 35 feet. The draft makes it 100 feet, with a 15-foot building setback beyond that. On this lot that leaves at most about a fifth of the ground, outside the band, for anything new: an addition toward the creek, a shop, a shed or an ADU would have to fit there, behind the setback.

The house stays. Under existing code, a home damaged by fire or any other cause keeps its grandfathered status only if a permit application is filed within six months.

Across the city, 1,200 of the touched lots are homes and 178 are undeveloped lots. On 879 of all the touched lots the band covers more than half the ground.

Lot figures come from County parcel records and the City's published 100-foot band, the same result the checker above returns. The owner is not named.

Our own Planning Commission asked for a second look.

On Sept 14, after 13 residents testified, the Planning Commission voted 7-0 to recommend that Council have staff revise the draft using science matching local conditions. The Oct 9 hearing draft still proposes a flat 100 feet on every creek.

No state law sets 100 feet. The state Department of Fish and Wildlife's own letter on this draft says a site evaluation "would be best." On Oct 14 Council heard from residents and asked staff to bring back options no earlier than Nov 4 and by the end of November. A few words from you still help.

Healthy creeks and buildable lots are not a trade. Every named creek in town is fish-bearing; the question is the right width for each creek, not whether to protect it.

Mill Creek is the exception. Its concrete flood channel through town stays under the City's Shoreline Master Program, with a 35-foot flood-channel band today; the draft does not change it.

What the record actually says

Same creeks. Ten yardsticks. One number.

Here is every standard the City's own record points to for how wide a creek buffer should be. Then look at the one the draft picked.

  1. The City

    100 ft

    "The first 100 feet from a stream is the minimum distance for effective pollution removal."

    City CAO webpage ↗

  2. State Fish & Wildlife

    Tree height

    Site potential tree height "should be the standard for buffer widths, with our online mapping tool being the best way" to set them.

    WDFW letter, Aug 28, 2026 ↗

  3. The same letter

    Go look

    "Table 6 is recommending 100 ft, which may be compliant… however a site evaluation would be best."

    WDFW letter, Aug 28, 2026 ↗

  4. The same letter, again

    Never under 100

    Averaging should not reduce a buffer "below 100 feet at its narrowest point." The draft lets averaging go down to 75 feet.

    WDFW letter; draft table ↗

  5. Walla Walla County

    35 · 50 · 75 · 100

    The County's own minimum riparian buffer map sets different widths for different creeks.

    County GIS layer, Aug 31, 2026 ↗

  6. City Planning Commission, 7-0

    Local science

    Revise the draft "following available science matching local conditions."

    Draft minutes, Sept 14, 2026 ↗

  7. The draft itself

    Case by case

    For creek segments brought back out of pipes, buffers "will be determined on a case-by-case basis based on a site-specific analysis."

    Draft table, p. 47 ↗

  8. Same chapter, wetlands

    25 to 250 ft

    In the same chapter of City code, wetland buffers are tiered by wetland category and land use, from 25 feet to 250 feet. Creeks get one number.

    WWMC 21.04.340, Table 4 ↗

  9. What the City recorded

    14–31 ft

    The City's own recorded averages of streamside vegetation: Yellowhawk 31 ft, Titus 25, Garrison 24, Reser 23, Russell 21, Stone 20, Bryant 14. The draft deletes this table.

    Draft table, pp. 41–42 ↗

  10. Staff's label

    "State Mandated"

    The staff table files the stream-buffer section under "State Mandated," citing RCW 36.70A.172. That law requires best available science. It sets no width.

    Read RCW 36.70A.172 ↗

Read the City's own record

Worth a second look.

Ten places where the City's own documents point in different directions. We share them in good faith, so Council can sort them out before it votes. Every line is quoted and linked.

  1. 01

    Storm drains skip the buffer.

    The City says the first 100 feet is "the minimum distance for effective pollution removal."

    The City's own handout says storm-drain water "flows directly into the many creeks running through the city… It isn't treated."

    City CAO page ↗ · City "Stormwater 101" ↗

  2. 02

    The City's science review maps 35 feet.

    The draft sets 100 feet on every creek.

    The only buffer map in the City's June 2026 science review, captioned "City of Walla Walla recommended FWHCA buffers," draws most city creeks at 35 feet and Yellowhawk at 50.

    BAS Review, Figure 12, p. 23 ↗

  3. 03

    Same night, two answers.

    The Comprehensive Plan (hearing 6C) says critical areas "do not significantly affect developable acreage."

    The critical areas draft (hearing 6E) nearly triples the stream-buffer area, from 269 to 757 acres, and touches about 1,490 city lots.

    Comp Plan 2046, App. III ↗ · Walla Walla impact

  4. 04

    "State Mandated," but the law sets no width.

    The draft labels the new 15-foot building setback "State Mandated."

    The City's consultant wrote only that the City "may consider" raising the setback from five feet to fifteen. The law cited sets no width.

    Gap Analysis, p. 8 ↗ · Draft table, p. 19 ↗

  5. 05

    The 2022 state bill would have spared small lots.

    Gov. Jay Inslee's 2022 bill exempted a parcel where the zone "would cover more than 50 percent of the parcel," and offered at least 70% cost-share. It never got a committee vote.

    The City's draft has neither. On the City's own map, 879 lots are more than half inside the band.

    HB 1838 (2022) ↗ · Walla Walla impact

  6. 06

    State comments shaped the draft. Our commission's vote hasn't yet.

    The staff memo says state Fish & Wildlife commented, "and changes were made to reflect the agency's comments."

    The Planning Commission voted 7-0 to revise the draft for local conditions. Since that vote, the Oct 9 draft changed one definition.

    Council memo, Item 6E ↗ · PC minutes ↗

  7. 07

    Pesticides are still allowed inside the buffer.

    The buffer is for "effective pollution removal," the City says.

    The draft still allows "the application of herbicides, pesticides, organic or mineral-derived fertilizers" inside the buffer, with no City permit or report.

    Draft table, p. 14, §21.04.085(B)(12) ↗

  8. 08

    In a pipe: 0 feet. Between pipes: 100.

    City code the draft keeps: "No riparian buffers are required along segments of piped or culverted streams."

    The short open stretches between those pipes get 100 feet. A restored stretch gets "case-by-case… site-specific analysis."

    WWMC 21.04.670(B) ↗ · Draft table, p. 47 ↗

  9. 09

    Roads may cross the buffer. Additions must stay out.

    Roads, utility lines and stormwater outfalls "may be permitted" in stream buffers, and farming is exempt from the chapter.

    For a home, addition or garage: "all structures and activities shall be located outside of the riparian habitat buffers."

    WWMC 21.04.620 ↗ · Draft table, p. 39 ↗

  10. 10

    There is time: the law allows 12 months.

    Staff told the Planning Commission that missing Dec 31 would be a "direct violation of state law."

    State law says a city "fewer than 12 months out of compliance" on these rules is making "substantial progress." Council already has regular meetings set for Nov 4 and Nov 18.

    PC minutes, p. 5 ↗ · RCW 36.70A.130(7) ↗

Why is this happening?

No law sets 100 feet.
The Legislature declined to.

  1. 1990

    Growth Management Act, signed by Gov. Booth Gardner. Cities must protect "critical areas." No buffer widths.

  2. 1995

    Gov. Mike Lowry's bill adds "include the best available science." Still no widths.

  3. 2018–20

    State Fish & Wildlife publishes its riparian guidance. Volume 2 calls itself "not, in and of itself, science" but "policy recommendations."

  4. 2022

    Gov. Jay Inslee asks the Legislature to require those zones in city updates (HB 1838). It dies without a committee vote.

  5. 2023

    A voluntary version, also requested by the Governor, dies too.

  6. 2026

    Walla Walla's draft writes the agency's 100 feet into city code.

Council can adopt it, tier it by creek, or order the local study its own Planning Commission asked for. Staff and Council are working hard under a state deadline, and everyone wants healthy creeks. A little more time to measure ours would serve us all.

What the science review covers.

The City's June 2026 science review says its purpose is "not to replicate the County's work, but to supplement it." It contains no field survey of Walla Walla's creeks. State Fish & Wildlife's own science volume says "no widely accepted recommendations have emerged on minimum buffer widths needed to protect water quality."

City BAS Review, June 26, 2026 ↗ · WDFW Riparian Ecosystems Vol. 1, p. 141 ↗

The ask, in four points

  1. Honor the Planning Commission's 7-0 recommendation of Sept 14: revise the draft before adopting it.
  2. Get a locally scoped best-available-science review.
  3. Replace the single 100-foot width with tiered buffers that reflect each creek's condition and fish use, and publish a parcel and housing-capacity analysis first.
  4. Protect existing homes in writing: repairs, additions and ADUs on the side away from the creek, and rebuilding after fire without losing grandfathered status in six months under existing code.
