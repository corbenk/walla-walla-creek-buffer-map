From the same public record

More voices from the record.

Twelve written comments are attached to the Council’s October 14 agenda item, and thirteen people testified at the Planning Commission hearing on September 14. Of the members of the public and two agencies who addressed the stream buffer, sixteen ask the Council to revise the flat 100-foot width, pause, or add relief; one is conditional; one spoke largely in favor; the state fish and wildlife agency supports the draft and asks for stricter terms. The cards below quote each document and link to it. Private residents are shown by first name and last initial until CreekChecker has reached them.

James K. Hayner, whose memo appears on this page, also filed a one-page map at the September 14 hearing (Council packet p. 1089). Its legend states “1400 lots and/or parcels affected” and “over 1000 acres”; those are his figures, not the City consultant’s, whose July memo puts the proposed buffer area at 757.4 acres.

Matt Heuer

Government Affairs Director · Walla Walla Valley Association of REALTORS

October 8, 2026 · Original document · 12 pages

“Decision-makers should be expressly told that 100 feet is a City policy choice informed by a WDFW recommendation, not a buffer width imposed by RCW 36.70A.172.”

Matt Heuer · letter, p. 3 (packet p. 1097)

Read the original letter ↗
Three points he raises · sources
To
City of Walla Walla City Council
Re
2026 Critical Areas Ordinance Update, File PLN5-26-0001 - Technical, Legal, and Policy Concerns
  1. A policy choice, not a state width

    Argues state law requires using best available science, not any particular width, and that labeling 100 ft 'State Mandated' blurs that distinction.

From City staff and the Council memo, not a citizen comment

What the City said.

The staff report to the Planning Commission (p. 10) says the riparian buffers “are proposed to increase from 35 or 50 feet, depending on the waterway, to 100 feet” and places the change under “a state mandate under RCW … 36.70A.172,” the law that requires best available science. Asked at the September 14 hearing what would happen if the amendments were not adopted by December 31, 2026, staff said the City “would be in direct violation of state law” (draft minutes, p. 5). The Council memo for the October 14 hearing states that “comments were received from the Washington State Department of Fish and Wildlife, and changes were made to reflect the agency’s comments,” and staff recommend that the Council open the public hearing and receive testimony. Several of the comments above dispute the “mandate” description; the rules and choices section separates the state requirement from the City proposal, and the two-minute brief shows what state law does and does not say.

Council takes up staff’s options in November. Say what you want the Council to consider, in your own words; a line or two about your own lot is enough.
