How we computed the buffer numbers

Computed 2026-10-10 from the City of Walla Walla's and Walla Walla County's own public GIS services. Nothing here is an official determination; see the disclaimer on the map.

Read this map in three parts

The band comes from the City's mapped stream centerlines. Your lot comes from County parcel records. The rule comes from the draft ordinance. A map comparison helps you ask a better question; a site survey and the City determine the regulated line.

What the map shows

The photographic background is USDA / USGS aerial imagery from The National Map. Image dates vary; these are not live photographs. Switch to Street map for a simpler background. The choice changes only the background: City and County GIS still supply the bands, boundaries and parcel results.

  • Stream centerlines and the 100-ft buffer band are read live, every time the page loads, from the City of Walla Walla GIS Group's public map service WW_Streams_Riparian_Buffer (published 2026-10-06). The 100-ft band is the City's own polygon, named by the City "Visible Streams Buffer 100 Feet From Stream Centerline."
  • The 35, 50, 75 and 150-ft comparison bands are computed in your browser by offsetting the same City centerlines (Turf.js). They are for comparison only.
  • Parcels are read live, one at a time, from Walla Walla County's public Tax Parcels service. The share of a parcel inside the band is computed in your browser from the parcel polygon and the band polygon.
  • No City or County geometry is copied to this website.

Where the rules change: city, County, College Place, Mill Creek

  • You are in: every check first locates the point. Inside the City's own limit rings (even-odd test on the City's 2026-10-06 limit layer; it reproduces the City's 9,029-acre figure exactly) the proposed CAO band applies. Otherwise the County's City_Limits and Urban_Growth_Areas polygons decide between College Place, a UGA (unincorporated, County rules) and unincorporated county.
  • Outside city limits the card tests the point and lot against Walla Walla County's working riparian buffer layer (published 2026-08-31; tiered 35/50/75/100 ft per reach via its buff_dist attribute) and names the nearest County-mapped stream. The County has not published a draft ordinance or hearing dates; its periodic update is due 2026-12-31. The layer is a preview, not a rule.
  • College Place has its own critical-areas code and no public buffer layer; the map shows only its boundary.
  • Mill Creek is excluded from the draft CAO ("See SMP"). It is drawn from the County hydrography layer (name MILL CREEK / MILL CREEK (CHANNEL)) for the in-city reach, with the Shoreline Master Program's 35-ft flood-channel buffer (SMP Table 6.5-1, Gose St to the Bennington Lake diversion) and the 200-ft shoreline jurisdiction noted on the card. We do not claim SMP widths for reaches outside the flood channel; the SMP PDF could not be text-extracted for this check (see research/05).

Reconciling with the City consultant's figures

The City's consultant (Facet, July 2026) reports the buffer area growing from 268.8 to 757.4 acres on 43.2 stream miles (29.7 open + 13.5 piped). The map's band is 477 acres on 17.9 miles.

These are both the City's numbers, and they measure different things. Facet's stream network is roughly 2.4 times longer than the City's Oct 6 "visible streams" layer: it includes piped reaches (13.5 miles), and, by its length, additional open reaches that the visible-streams layer does not draw (the USGS National Hydrography Dataset shows about 36 miles of flowlines inside city limits, and County hydrography about 28). 43.2 miles buffered 100 ft on each side is about 1,047 acres before accounting for overlaps and the city boundary, which is consistent with Facet's 757 acres inside the city. The map band should therefore be read as the minimum extent on mapped open channels; the consultant's figure is the broader estimate the City is working from.

A figure of "1,100 acres" that has circulated is not in any City document we found; 42 miles corresponds to Facet's 43.2.

Caveats

  • Centerline vs. ordinary high-water mark. Every band on this map, including the City's own, is measured from a digitized stream centerline. The draft ordinance measures from the ordinary high-water mark, top of bank, or channel migration zone, whichever is greater. For a channel 20 to 60 ft wide, the regulated edge is 10 to 30 ft farther out than drawn. That is why the result card flags lots within 150 ft as "possibly affected."
  • Positional accuracy. The City's centerlines are attributed "Placed on Map" (digitized from imagery, not surveyed). Expect tens of feet of uncertainty; a result within about 30 ft of a threshold should be treated as undetermined.
  • Which streams. The City layer draws visible open channels inside city limits only: it omits Mill Creek, piped reaches, and all streams in the unincorporated Urban Growth Area, although the draft ordinance also covers "intermittent open channels between piped sections" and the UGA. The City's layer contains every creek named in draft Table 6 except Reser Creek, which lies outside city limits; it also includes Butcher, Lincoln, College, Barber and Kathy creeks, three springs and one short irrigation segment under the draft's 'and others within city limits' language, and it excludes Mill Creek (Shoreline Master Program). Audit (2026-10-10, data/scripts/audit_streams.py): 61 segments, 17.94 miles, all inside city limits; one segment flagged intermittent. Outside city limits the map returns "outside city limits" rather than a distance: we deliberately do not add County or State hydrography for the growth area, because it is different data under different (County) rules.
  • Parcel data is the County Assessor's working layer (2026-09-28). Owner names are never requested or displayed.
  • Only the City of Walla Walla can determine whether a specific property is subject to critical-areas rules. Confirm with Development Services (509-524-4710, permits@wallawallawa.gov).

Optional comparison · not a forecast

What if a 100‑foot buffer applied here?

The separate what-if switch helps residents outside city limits see the size of a wider band. It does not show an adopted County rule, establish a pending County proposal or predict that one will pass. The County’s 2026 periodic-update project charter, page 5, includes a critical-areas update; it does not establish a blanket 100-foot width.

Today and proposed: the current City code, Table 6, lists 35-foot buffers for most named creek reaches and 50 feet for the listed Yellowhawk reach, subject to exceptions. The City draft proposes 100 feet per side and a separate 15-foot building setback. Unincorporated land and College Place have their own applicable rules. The County’s working buffer GIS layer is a separate reference, not proof of an adopted rule.

How the preview is drawn: on request, the browser reads the County’s live Hydrologic Features and City Limits data. It computes 100 feet on each side of mapped centerlines, clips away incorporated cities, and excludes named Mill Creek reaches. The dashed red overlay is an illustration; no 15-foot setback is added. The geometry stays in your browser.

Coverage: this preview covers the valley rectangle from 118.52° W to 118.14° W and 45.96° N to 46.16° N, the same working area used for the map’s County context. It is not countywide or statewide. Geometry is generalized to roughly 2 meters by the service, as in the existing County context layers. The preview compares only the selected point, not the whole parcel. A blank area can reflect missing mapped waterways and does not establish that a property is unregulated. Other shoreline reaches, wetlands and site-specific exceptions are not evaluated.

What a wider rule could mean: if adopted in your jurisdiction, a wider buffer could affect the room available for new structures, additions and subdivisions. Actual restrictions, existing-use protections and exceptions would depend on the adopted wording and a site determination. Centerlines do not locate the ordinary high-water mark, top of bank or channel migration zone used in regulatory measurements. Ask the responsible planning office for the rule and measurement that apply to your lot.

The regular property result and City statistics do not change when this overlay is on. The 35/50/75/100/150 buttons still control only the City band. A copied link retains an enabled scenario as #whatif-100, and the hypothetical label remains visible in the map key.

Privacy & civic purpose

Built by residents. For residents.

CreekChecker is a resident-built, unpaid civic project. We help Washington State property owners see how proposed rules would affect their land, using the governments' own data. We believe residents deserve a meaningful say before government limits how they can use their land.

We are residents, not the City. We show the proposal, the public data and the limits of what the map can tell you, so you can check the sources, form your own view and speak for yourself. We want the Council to get the local science before adopting a width; the map itself only shows what the governments' own data say.

Our privacy promise

We will never sell your personal data. We don’t collect your address searches into a CreekChecker database. No account or email signup is required to use the map. We use Google Analytics to count visits and pages. We configure it to drop the map's location parameters, so the places you look up are not sent. We do not store the addresses you type. It is skipped when your browser sends Global Privacy Control or Do Not Track, and in Safari, which blocks it. The map runs in your browser.

How we count neighbors

The counter at the top of each page counts actions, not people: the first time a browser finds a Walla Walla County lot with an address search, opens an email to Council from this site, or taps “I’ll be there Oct 14.” Each browser counts once per action, and each network has a daily limit. We keep a random ID for your browser and a one-way code for your network, never your IP address, your address search or your email; for a lot check we also note the name of the nearest creek. The counts are not verified, and the lot count began with the property owners who tested the map before launch.

Optional contact information

The contact form is separate from the map. It asks for your name, your email address and, if you choose, a phone number. It also asks about your property, your concerns and whether you want updates and action alerts about this proposal. Sending it lets the organizers reply about this proposal. Submissions go to a Google Form owned by the organizers and are stored in a Google Sheet; Google's privacy policy applies. A result can open a relevant reading guide using a broad category in the page fragment; no address or parcel is included. We do not attach your property result, parcel, address searches or map coordinates to the contact form, and we will never sell your personal data.

Sending the contact form is not a petition signature. There is no petition and no signature list. If a signature form were ever opened, it would explain its final request, recipient and treatment of names before anyone signs.

What happens when you use the map

  • Finding your lot: your browser sends address or parcel searches and map locations directly to Walla Walla County’s ArcGIS service. If a parcel cannot be found, the address may be sent to OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim search service. Some ZIP lookups also use Nominatim.
  • Loading the site: hosting, map, imagery, font and script providers receive normal connection information, such as your IP address, and may keep their own service logs. Map providers receive requests for the area you view. Their handling of those requests is governed by their own policies.
  • On your device: the site remembers your disclaimer acknowledgement, first-visit completion, layer choices and aerial/street preference in browser storage. It does not save a search-history database. You can clear these preferences through your browser’s site-data controls.
  • Location and sharing: “Find my location” requires your browser’s location permission. A selected point’s coordinates and map settings appear in the page URL and copied links; they can remain in browser history and be seen by anyone you share the link with.
  • Emailing the Council: the button opens a draft in your email app. Nothing is sent automatically. If you send it, the City and your email provider receive the information you include.

Our promise describes what this project does. It does not mean that using an online map makes no requests to outside services.
