State requirement · City proposal · Your say

What is required.
What can still change.

The Walla Walla City proposal raises questions about science, existing homes and funding. Start with the distinction between a legal requirement and a particular draft.

Sources checked October 10, 2026. This section concerns Walla Walla City; County and other city rules need their own review.

Does the state require 100 feet everywhere?

State law requires best available science. It does not set a universal 100-foot width. RCW 36.70A.172 requires cities and counties to include that science when protecting critical areas, with special consideration for fish that migrate between fresh and salt water.

The City cites Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife guidance in support of its proposed minimum 100-foot buffer. Choosing another approach still needs a defensible record. State guidance says a departure from science-based recommendations should identify supporting information, explain the reasoning, and address risks and measures to limit them.

RCW 36.70A.172 ↗WAC 365-195-915 ↗The City’s explanation ↗

A useful question: What evidence supports the proposed width on each creek, and how were alternatives evaluated?

Would this mean I cannot repair or rebuild?

A blanket “no rebuilding” claim leaves out the conditions. Existing City code allows certain maintenance, repair and reconstruction of lawful nonconforming structures. For a damaged nonconforming structure, the code includes a six-month deadline to apply for a permit to repair, rebuild or replace it. Other conditions and permits can also apply.

Repairing a home, expanding it and constructing a new building are different questions. Read the property guide for the draft’s wording and the existing-code provisions, then ask the City for a written answer about your planned work.

Existing critical-areas code ↗Nonconforming structures · Chapter 20.212 ↗Read the repair and rebuilding guide →

A useful question: Which section governs my project, what permits would I need, and what deadline applies?

Does the City lose funding if it changes this draft?

Funding consequences are tied to compliance and program rules. The Growth Management Act provides for financial sanctions through specified findings and procedures. That is different from an automatic loss of all state funding if this particular draft is changed or rejected.

Some funding programs also have their own eligibility requirements. For example, public-works assistance generally requires adoption of the plans and development regulations required by the GMA, with statutory exceptions. The City should identify the specific program, requirement, deadline and amount at risk when making a funding claim.

RCW 36.70A.340 · sanctions ↗RCW 36.70A.345 · findings ↗RCW 43.155.070 · public-works eligibility ↗

A useful question: Which funding source is at risk, under what rule, and would a compliant alternative protect eligibility?

Elsewhere in Washington

Other towns faced the same line.

Four Washington communities and one Oregon town drew a line like this one. Residents ran into the questions Walla Walla is asking now: what happens to a house already inside the band, to a lot bought before the rule, or to a home that burns. What held up was not “no buffer.” It was a buffer that fits the lot, measured per creek, with rebuild rights written in.

Sources checked October 12, 2026. Each story links to the public record it comes from; one family is not named at their privacy.

Puyallup, Washington · 2025

A whole lot inside the line, on a creek with no water in it.

When Puyallup proposed raising non-fish stream buffers from 35–50 feet to 100, homeowners filled the Aug. 13, 2025 Planning Commission hearing. One said the rule would put his whole lot inside a 100-foot buffer on a creek with no water in it. Another said the new line would overlap his house and yard. The commission ended up holding fish-stream buffers at 100 feet instead of staff’s 150.

For Walla Walla: Walla Walla’s draft makes the same 35-to-100 jump on the creeks it covers.

Source: Puyallup Planning Commission minutes, Aug. 13, 2025 ↗ · outcome ↗

Newcastle, Washington · 2025–26

Five hundred letters, and a question about the house after a fire.

Newcastle mailed about 500 letters to owners near streams and wetlands before widening some fish-stream buffers from 100 to 150 feet. The city’s own FAQ says existing buildings are grandfathered, but if a building is removed, the lot or replacement building “may need to meet current code.” Council adopted Ordinance 2026-683 on Feb. 17, 2026 with a reinstated setback and limited rebuild rights on fully covered lots.

For Walla Walla: Rebuilding after a fire is the same open question for Walla Walla’s existing homes.

Source: City of Newcastle, 2025 Critical Areas Update ↗ · Ordinance 2026-683 ↗

Talent, Oregon · 2021

Fire survivors, a 35-foot buffer, and no way to rebuild.

Six months after the Almeda Fire, eight to ten Talent families still could not rebuild because their lots sat in a floodway or the city’s 35-foot creek buffer, and the old homes could not be grandfathered. “The Almeda Fire was six months ago and we want to go home,” one resident told the council. The city moved to an emergency ordinance so those families could rebuild, while keeping the buffer as riparian protection.

For Walla Walla: Even a 35-foot buffer blocked rebuilding without an explicit rebuild rule.

Source: KTVL, Mar. 4, 2021 ↗ · follow-up ↗

Spokane Valley, Washington · 1998

Twenty thousand dollars spent, then the permit window closed.

After Spokane County adopted a flat 250-foot river buffer in 1996, a Valley pharmacist who had owned his riverfront lot since 1983 spent about $20,000 on a drainfield, utilities and plans for a log home, then missed the permit window. Two neighbors had permits for side-by-side homes denied. More than 100 owners pooled money for a scientist’s study to support smaller, site-specific buffers.

For Walla Walla: That is the ask in Walla Walla: measure each creek instead of one number for all.

Source: Spokesman-Review, Oct. 29, 1998, “Buffer Beef” ↗ · historical case; owners not contacted (research/09 §E)

Thurston County, Washington · 2024–25

Sixteen months and a mitigation plan to replace a small old house.

A Thurston County family on 0.83 acres with an 800-square-foot 1930s house too small for them applied in April 2024 to replace it. The wetland buffer covered the existing footprint. They needed a critical-areas report, a reasonable-use exception, a buffer mitigation plan and a drainage plan. Approval came 16 months later, in August 2025, conditioned on 3,500 square feet of plantings and five years of monitoring.

For Walla Walla: The exception works; it costs a year and a half and a consultant.

Source: Thurston County Hearing Examiner decision, Aug. 4, 2025 · family not named at their privacy; decision on file with the County

What they adopted instead

From the adopted codes · research notes section D

  1. Skagit County · Nov. 25, 2025

    Fixed 100- and 150-foot widths instead of the state tree-height model, which county staff called “unpredictable, costly, hard to enforce.” Ongoing farming continues inside buffers, and the reasonable-use exception was streamlined.

    Skagit County critical-areas code, as amended Nov. 25, 2025 · Salish Current, Nov. 26, 2025 ↗
  2. Mill Creek, Washington · Jan. 6, 2026

    Adopted the state riparian zones but wrote in that nonconforming structures may be “maintained, altered and rebuilt,” allowed redevelopment within the existing impervious footprint, and let applicants commission their own site study.

    Mill Creek Municipal Code Ch. 18.06 · City SEPA determination, Dec. 2025 ↗
  3. Kitsap County · Ord. 633-2024

    Smaller alternative widths inside urban growth areas (150 feet on fish streams, 75 feet on non-fish streams) for infill lots, plus an administrative 25–50 percent reduction for single-family homes through a Type II review.

    Kitsap County Code Title 19 (19.100, 19.300) · KCC 19.100 ↗
  4. Newcastle, Washington · Ord. 2026-683, Feb. 17, 2026

    Grandfathered existing buildings, reinstated a building setback, allowed limited rebuilding on fully covered lots, and published a public stream-buffer dashboard.

    Newcastle Municipal Code Ch. 18.24 · Ordinance 2026-683 ↗
The other side, honestly

Buffers do real work, and courts have said so. The Department of Ecology’s 2004 floodplain-management report found that stream buffers adopted under critical-areas codes had helped reduce flood losses, and called building setbacks one of the most effective floodplain practices in the state. Washington courts upheld wide buffers in Kitsap County in 2011 and in Jefferson County in 2017, and the state Supreme Court declined to review the Jefferson case. We do not dispute the science or the law. The question here is narrower: what a fixed width does to a lot that is already house, driveway and lawn. Our position stays the same: a buffer that fits the lot, measured per creek, with rebuild rights. Never “no buffer.”

Ecology, Floodplain Management in Washington (2004) ↗Kitsap Alliance v. Growth Board (2011) ↗Olympic Stewardship Foundation (2017) ↗
